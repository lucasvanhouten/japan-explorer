# Getting around Japan — the know-how that changes plans

Practical mechanics, sourced. Every number below carries a link; schedules and prices do change, so
confirm anything a plan actually depends on. Terms in Japanese are explained the first time they
appear.

---

## The shape of the network

Japan's long-distance backbone is the **shinkansen** (新幹線, the high-speed rail network, commonly
called the bullet train). The busiest line, the Tōkaidō, links Tokyo, Nagoya, Kyoto and Osaka and
continues west as the Sanyō line to Hiroshima and Fukuoka; separate lines run north to Sendai and
Morioka, and along the Sea of Japan coast to Kanazawa and Fukui. Tokyo to Kyoto is about
**2h15 with no changes**, several departures an hour
([japan-guide](https://www.japan-guide.com/e/e2363.html)); Kyoto to Hiroshima about **1h50**
([japan-guide](https://www.japan-guide.com/e/e3404.html)); Kyoto to Fukuoka about **2h40–3h**
([japan-guide](https://www.japan-guide.com/e/e4802.html)).

Two things follow. Distances are smaller than a map suggests, so trips fail from too many stops
rather than from geography. And the network is a spine: almost every countryside inn
means getting onto the spine, riding it, then transferring to a local line, a bus, or a car for the
last stretch — which is where plans go wrong, and what most of this document is about.

One recent change worth knowing: the coastal Hokuriku line was extended to Tsuruga in March 2024,
which **ended the one-seat ride between Kyoto and Kanazawa**. That journey is now about **2h15 with
one cross-platform change at Tsuruga** — signposted and easy, but a change
([Visit Kanazawa](https://visitkanazawa.jp/en/know-before-you-go/detail_416.html)).

## The rail pass question

The **Japan Rail Pass** is a fixed-price national pass. Since the October 2023 increase the 7-day
ordinary pass costs **¥50,000** (14-day ¥80,000, 21-day ¥100,000) — up from ¥29,650, which is why
older advice reads so differently ([japan-guide](https://www.japan-guide.com/e/e2361.html)).

The pass **now allows the fastest trains** — the Nozomi and Mizuho services it used to exclude — but
with a per-journey surcharge bought at a rail office: about ¥4,960 Tokyo–Kyoto, ¥6,500
Tokyo–Hiroshima, ¥8,140 Tokyo–Fukuoka
([JapanRailPass.net](https://japanrailpass.net/en/use/special-ticket/)). Any page still saying you
cannot ride a Nozomi with the pass is out of date.

**The arithmetic, plainly.** The pass wins when three or more long high-speed journeys land inside
one seven-day window — something like Tokyo to Kanazawa to Kyoto to Hiroshima to Fukuoka in a week.
It loses on a slow trip: a two-week itinerary with long stays and countryside stops spreads its big
journeys too thinly, and the surcharge on the fast trains erodes what is left. For those trips,
**individual tickets are usually cheaper and always more flexible**
([guide2japan](https://www.guide2japan.com/plan-a-trip/jr-pass/)). Add the journeys up at real
fares before buying anything.

**Regional passes are the better deal more often than people expect**, because they are priced for
one area: the JR Kyushu pass (covers Fukuoka, Nagasaki, Kagoshima and the Yufuin scenic express),
JR West's Sanyō–San'in and Kansai–Hiroshima passes, and the Hokuriku Arch Pass for a
Tokyo–Kanazawa–Kyoto loop including that Tsuruga change
([jrpass blog](https://www.jrpass.com/blog/explore-western-japan-on-jr-thunderbird)). Price the
regional pass for the region you are actually in.

## Seat reservations

Most shinkansen have unreserved as well as reserved cars, so on a quiet weekday you can turn up.
Three exceptions catch people out:

- **Some trains are reserved-only, with no unreserved cars at all** — including the fastest services
  on the northern and coastal lines, the Hayabusa to Sendai and Morioka and the Kagayaki to
  Kanazawa. Without a reservation you cannot board them
  ([Snow Monkey](https://www.snowmonkeyresorts.com/access/hokuriku-shinkansen/)).
- **Some scenic limited expresses are fully reserved, mandatory even with a rail pass.** The Yufuin
  no Mori, from Fukuoka to the hot-spring town of Yufuin, runs about three times a day outbound and
  opens reservations one month ahead at 10:00 Japan time, costing roughly ¥1,500 on top of a pass
  ([matcha](https://matcha-jp.com/en/19455)).
- **Rural limited expresses can run two to five times a day**, and some don't go all the way to the
  end of the line. Only about six of the fourteen daily services toward the coastal hot-spring town
  of Kinosaki actually reach it; the rest stop short
  ([osakastation](https://www.osakastation.com/the-limited-express-kounotori-for-fukuchiyama-and-kinosaki-onsen/)).
  Check the specific departure, not just the route.

**How to book from abroad.** The official app for the Tōkaidō, Sanyō and Kyushu lines is **smartEX**:
reservations open about a month out, seat changes are free before departure, and you board with a QR
code ([smartEX](https://smart-ex.jp/en/beginner/)). Note that the northern and coastal lines
(Sendai, Kanazawa) belong to a different railway company and are booked through **eki-net** instead.

Foreign credit cards on smartEX are hit-or-miss: its security check and ¥1 verification charge get
rejected by some banks, with reported success on American Express and many Visa/Mastercard products
and repeated failures on others
([FlyerTalk](https://www.flyertalk.com/forum/japan/2137810-smartex-frustrating-experience.html)).
**Register the card weeks before the trip**, not at the station. Fallbacks: eki-net, a resale
platform, or the machines and staffed offices in any major station, which work fine.

## Send your luggage ahead

The single highest-value logistics move in Japan. **Takkyubin** (宅急便, door-to-door parcel delivery,
the largest operator being Yamato Transport, whose black-cat logo is everywhere) will move a
suitcase from one hotel to the next, typically **overnight between major cities**, for roughly
**¥1,600 for a small bag up to about ¥3,680 for a large suitcase** — cheaper if you pay
electronically ([MailMate](https://mailmate.jp/blog/yamato-transport-airport-luggage-delivery-service)).
Hokkaido and Okinawa take two to three days
([MailMate](https://mailmate.jp/blog/japan-luggage-delivery)).

Arrange it at the hotel front desk, which keeps the forms and fills them in for you; airports have
their own counters, and one first-hand account reports every hotel handling it with the luggage in
the next room the following day
([MIT-MISTI](https://misti.mit.edu/japan-preparation-and-training/japan-logistics/japan-takkyubin-sending-luggage-within-japan)).
Send the big bags a day ahead and travel with an overnight daypack — it is what makes crowded trains,
cross-platform transfers and bus-served hot-spring towns pleasant instead of grim. Hakone-Yumoto,
gateway to the Hakone hot springs, even has a counter that delivers straight to your inn
([Hakone Navi](https://www.hakonenavi.jp/international/en/station/yumoto)).

**It also sidesteps the oversized-baggage rule.** On the Tōkaidō, Sanyō, Kyushu and Nishi-Kyushu
lines, a bag whose length plus width plus height totals **160–250 cm** needs a specific seat with
luggage space behind it, and boarding without that reservation carries a **¥1,000 penalty**
([smartEX](https://smart-ex.jp/en/entraining/oversized-baggage/),
[japantrain](https://japantrain.net/guide/shinkansen-oversized-baggage-rules)). A typical large
checked suitcase, around 150–158 cm, falls just under. The rule does **not** apply on the northern or
coastal lines, which have onboard luggage areas instead
([japan-bullettrain](https://www.japan-bullettrain.com/articles/blog/shinkansen-luggage-size)).

## Paying for local transport

An **IC card** (a rechargeable stored-value card — Suica in the Tokyo area, ICOCA in Kansai, mutually
interchangeable) tapped at the gate covers subways, buses, local trains and convenience stores
nationwide. A chip shortage restricted sales of the physical cards for a while; **unrestricted sales
resumed in March 2025**, though availability still varies by station
([Essential Japan](https://essential-japan.com/news/suica-ic-cards-resume-unrestricted-sales-this-march/)).

**On an iPhone, add a Suica to Apple Wallet before you fly** — no app needed, top up in Wallet, no
queue on arrival. JR East also launched a mobile "Welcome Suica" for visitors in March 2025, valid
180 days. Android users generally still need a physical card
([LiveJapan](https://livejapan.com/en/in-tokyo/in-pref-tokyo/in-tokyo_train_station/article-a0005633/)).

## Winter

Snow risk is concentrated and predictable. The higher-risk lines are the **coastal Hokuriku line and
the Thunderbird express** serving Kanazawa, the **northern Tōhoku line** to Sendai and beyond — much
worse on its narrow-gauge branches toward Yamagata and Akita — and the **San'in coast** run to
Kinosaki. The Tōkaidō, Sanyō and Kyushu lines are largely snow-robust
([Snow Monkey](https://www.snowmonkeyresorts.com/access/hokuriku-shinkansen/),
[Tokyo Cheapo](https://tokyocheapo.com/travel/transport/tohoku-shinkansen-guide/)).

The practical read: on the high-speed lines themselves, winter delays are **possible rather than
routine**; local feeder lines, mountain buses and roads are the fragile part. Two rules follow.
**Never schedule a same-day onward flight after a winter journey through a snow region** — put a
night in between. And treat mountain roads as weather-dependent: highland routes like the cross-Kyushu
bus over Makinoto Pass at 1,330 m, or the roads around the Aso caldera, can add half an hour or close
outright, and driving them in midwinter wants proper winter tyres and a check of conditions that
morning.

Islands are their own category. Ferries and jetfoils cancel on sea state, and the small propeller
flights to islands like Yakushima cancel in poor weather and may run only once a day. Where both an
air and a sea route exist, the more frequent one is the safer plan even if it looks slower on paper.

## The last stretch: inn shuttles, buses and taxis

**Ask the inn how to arrive, and read its own access page.** This is the most reliable single source
for the final leg, and it is where the real constraints live. Typical patterns from inn access pages:

- **Free shuttles with fixed slots, not on-demand** — typically a small van meeting set afternoon
  times between roughly 14:00 and 18:00, one or two morning departures back, and **reservation
  required, often by the day before**. Miss the window and you are paying for a taxi.
- **Longer complimentary transfers from a main station**, 25–40 minutes, sometimes included in the
  rate — the shape used by inns well off the rail network.
- **No shuttle at all**, with a published fixed-fare taxi instead: around ¥5,440 for a 25-minute run
  from a prefectural capital's station is typical.
- **A bus as the only public option**, two or three a day, with a late-afternoon last departure that
  governs the day's timing. The cross-Kyushu bus linking Beppu, Yufuin, Kurokawa, Aso
  and Kumamoto is the classic case: about 1h45 Yufuin to Kurokawa, two services a day each way,
  reservation advised, final uphill departure 16:55
  ([timetable](https://japanbusonline.com/en/CourseSearch/14301010001),
  [Kurokawa access](https://www.kurokawaonsen.or.jp/en/access/)).

**Short inn-to-inn hops: take a car, not the train.** For neighbouring inns roughly under 50 km
apart, a chartered taxi or the inn's car is what a guest actually does, and it is usually far faster
— a 40-minute drive over a ridge becomes an hour and a half by train once you backtrack to the
nearest station and out again. Ask either inn to arrange it. The crossover is around 110 km: beyond
that the shinkansen matches or beats the car, and nobody takes a 145 km taxi. When the
destination is a big city, take the train regardless — the station is downtown, and the car isn't
faster once you hit traffic.

## Airports

**Tokyo has two.** **Haneda (HND)** is close in: about 13 minutes to Hamamatsuchō on the monorail, or
14–21 minutes to Shinagawa on the Keikyū line for ¥300–330, so roughly half an hour to central Tokyo
([jrailpass](https://www.jrailpass.com/blog/haneda-airport-to-tokyo)). **Narita (NRT)** is far out:
the Narita Express takes **53–60 minutes** to Tokyo Station, with reserved seats and luggage space,
and is covered by a rail pass ([JR East](https://www.jreast.co.jp/en/multi/nex/)). All else equal,
Haneda is worth paying for.

**For Kyoto and Osaka, fly into Kansai (KIX).** The Haruka limited express reaches Kyoto in **70–75
minutes**, every 30 minutes; the standard reserved fare is about ¥3,500 but there is a **discounted
foreign-visitor ticket at roughly ¥2,200**, bought on its own — the old combined card-and-ticket
bundle was discontinued in September 2023
([jrailpass](https://www.jrailpass.com/blog/haruka-express-kansai-airport)). Osaka's second airport,
**Itami (ITM)**, handles domestic flights only, on the most frequent domestic route in the country to
Haneda at about 1h10.

**Regional airports turn a long backtrack into a short hop** — the main reason to think about them.
Each region has one, most are about 1h45–2h from Tokyo by air, and most sit 40–55 minutes from their
city by airport bus. Fukuoka's is the standout: **five to seven minutes and ¥260 by subway, two stops
from the main station**, the closest major-city airport to its own downtown
([Fukuoka Airport](https://www.fukuoka-airport.jp/en/access/subway.html)). Others worth knowing:
Kumamoto and Kagoshima for central and southern Kyushu, Nagasaki for the west, **Oita for Yufuin**
(airport bus about 55 minutes), Hiroshima for the Inland Sea, and Komatsu for Kanazawa and the Kaga
hot springs (about 45 minutes by airport bus, 15 by taxi from the nearest of those towns).

The planning use is simple: **choose the airport you fly home from last, after the route is set.**
Flying out of the region you end in, rather than doubling back to where you arrived, routinely saves
half a day — and if the international flight leaves Tokyo, a domestic hop into Haneda from almost any
regional airport costs about two hours of block time.
