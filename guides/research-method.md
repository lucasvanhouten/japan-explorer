# How to research a place to stay in Japan

*A working method. It is the one the inn catalogue in this kit was built with — a few
hundred properties read across Japanese booking sites, English forums, and the properties' own
pages — reduced to the parts that transfer. Use it when the kit's tables don't cover a stop, when
the budget sits below them, or when you simply want to check a recommendation before you pay for it.*

## 0. Start inside the kit

Before any search: **the kit already answers most stops.** `catalog/catalog.md` holds every inn in
the catalogue as one table; `data/hotels.md` holds every researched city hotel; `data/transit-legs.md`
holds every looked-up travel time; and the Stage 4 tables in the main file are the shortlist built
from all three. Read those first, say what they hold for the stop in front of you, and go outside
only for what they do not cover — then say that you did. Everything below is the method
for that gap; the tables come first.

## The shape of the problem

Japanese lodging is reviewed almost entirely in Japanese, by Japanese guests, on sites most
travellers never open. The English-language internet sees a thin, skewed slice of it: the famous
names, the ones with English booking pages, and whatever the aggregators rank highly this quarter.
So the default research move — search in English, read the top ten results — systematically returns
the best-marketed places rather than the best places, and tells you almost nothing about the ones
worth finding.

The fix is an order of operations. Read the Japanese crowd for **quality**, the property's own site
for **facts**, and English forums for **judgement**. Never take one source for all three.

## 1. The Japanese sites, for quality

Three sites, in a rough hierarchy by market position.

**Ikyu (一休.com)** is the upper end: luxury hotels and traditional inns. Every property carries an
overall score out of 5 and sub-ratings — typically room, meals, service, bath, cleanliness — plus
the number of reviews behind them. This is the single most useful number in Japanese lodging.

Calibrate it the way the Japanese crowd does, because the scale is compressed and generous:

| Ikyu score | What it means |
|---|---|
| 4.8–5.0 | Top of the market. A property people travel for. |
| 4.6–4.8 | Very good. The band most excellent inns actually sit in. |
| 4.4–4.6 | Good, with something not quite resolved — usually the food or the building. |
| 4.2–4.4 | Mixed. On a famous or expensive name, a warning. |
| Below 4.2 | Something is wrong, or the property is not what it charges for. |

Two adjustments. **Review count matters**: 4.9 from eight reviews is a rumour, 4.7 from two hundred
is a fact. And **new properties score high** for a year or two on novelty, then settle — a 2025
opening at 4.85 and a twenty-year-old inn at 4.75 are not obviously in that order.

**Rakuten Travel** and **Jalan** cover everything below Ikyu's tier — business hotels, mid-range
inns, regional places with no English presence at all — on the same 5-point shape with the same
sub-ratings. For anything under roughly $250 a night these are the sources to use. Their scales run
slightly tougher than Ikyu's: 4.5+ on Rakuten is strong.

**Read the distribution, never the anecdote.** One review describing a hair in the bath is noise; forty reviews with a bath sub-rating of
4.2 against a service sub-rating of 4.9 is a finding, and it tells you exactly what kind of night
you are buying. Look for:

- **the gap between sub-ratings** — where a property is strong and where it is coasting;
- **repeated words** across many reviews (the same complaint about breakfast, the same praise for
  one staff member's name);
- **the recent ones** — a chef change, a renovation, a sale to a chain resets everything above.

If you cannot read Japanese, the sub-rating numbers alone are worth the visit, and machine
translation handles review prose well enough to spot repetition.

## 2. The property's own site, for facts

Every fact that could change your booking lives on the property's own pages, and nowhere else is
reliable:

- **Tattoo policy.** Many communal baths still refuse guests with visible tattoos. Check the
  facility's own notice — and check the rule for **overnight guests**, which is often a different
  page from the one for day visitors. Free bath access for guests is not an exemption from the
  policy. Where there is a **private or bookable bath**, or a bath in the room, the house rule stops
  mattering; that is the reliable answer.
- **Children.** Inn child policies vary wildly and the best inns are frequently the strictest —
  adults-only, or nobody under twelve, or children in certain room types only. It is often stated
  only in Japanese, and it changes. Confirm by email.
- **Meals.** Whether the rate includes dinner and breakfast, what the kitchen actually cooks, and
  whether dietary requests are possible — inn menus are fixed weeks ahead, so a request made on
  arrival is too late.
- **The bath itself.** "Onsen" on an aggregator can mean a natural hot spring, heated tap water, or
  a bathtub with a view. The property says which.
- **How you actually book.** Most places take an ordinary online reservation. The exceptions matter:
  no online booking at all, a window that opens on a fixed date months ahead, bookable only by phone
  or through an agent, or a lottery. A listing that exists on a booking site but shows no available
  dates is **not** a booking channel — verify a live one before promising anything.

## 3. English forums, for judgement

**FlyerTalk** (its Japan and luxury-hotel forums) and long-form **TripAdvisor** reviews carry the
best English writing on Japanese lodging: people who have stayed at ten comparable properties and
will tell you how this one compares. Use them for exactly that — comparison and judgement — and not
for facts about the building, which are often years out of date. Weigh a reviewer with a track
record across many properties far above a first-timer's rave. Where the English connoisseurs and the
Japanese crowd disagree, say so rather than resolving it: the split is usually real and usually
about food.

## 4. Verify the negatives

"Closed", "fully booked", "referral only", "no English booking", "tattoos banned" — these are claims
like any other, and they are the dangerous kind, because excluding something feels safe and produces
no visible error. A false negative silently deletes the best option on the list. Before you drop a
property, open its own page and confirm the reason. Never let one property's verified restriction
spread to a neighbour by association.

## 5. Link the real place

When you note a property, link **its own site**, and where you want a map link, open Google Maps,
find the property itself, and copy that place's link. A search URL built from a romanised name plus
a town lands on the town centre or a same-named business more often than it lands on the property.

## 6. Vary the stays

If a trip has several stops, different properties at each is worth paying for — the stays are part
of the trip. Treat "extend the hotel we already have" as a cost rather than a free
option, and offer a different property alongside it.

## What counts as evidence

**Evidence:** first-hand stays; score distributions and sub-ratings across many reviews; the
property's own written policy; a reviewer with a comparable track record.

**Not evidence:** the property's own adjectives; a press release or a design-magazine feature; one
glowing review; an aggregator's one-word verdict; a score with no review count attached.

## How to write up a find

Give it in the same shape as a row in the kit's own tables, and no larger:

> **Name** · neighbourhood · one line of what makes it itself · the link · *rate unverified — check
> live for your dates*

Then say where you found it and how sure you are: "Rakuten 4.6 from 340 reviews, strongest on
breakfast, weakest on room size; I have not found an English review." A named property with an
honest caveat is a plan. A neighbourhood and a price range is not an answer.

## The floor, in any city

Below the design-and-luxury tier sits a reliable layer worth naming, because it means no stop is
ever unanswerable. In any Japanese city, roughly **$90–200** a night for two buys a clean, small,
immaculate, well-located room. The chains that deliver it: **Dormy Inn** (communal hot bath, often a
real hot spring, free late-night noodles), **Mitsui Garden**, **Candeo** (top-floor baths),
**Richmond**, **Daiwa Roynet**, **Sotetsu Fresa Inn**, the **JR station hotels** (Granvia, JR-East
Metropolitan — attached to the platforms, which on a moving trip beats every other feature), and
**OMO by Hoshino Resorts** at the design end. The difference between a $120 and a $400 room in Japan
is mostly size, breakfast and view — not cleanliness, safety or service. A modest budget can do a
whole trip in that tier and spend the saving on one inn night worth remembering.

*Rates and policies move constantly. Everything above is a method for checking, not a substitute for
checking.*
